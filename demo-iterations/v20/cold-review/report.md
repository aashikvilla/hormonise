# Cold-judge report — Harmonise candidate film

## Executive decision: Maybe

The film presents a coherent concept for adults navigating family pressure: privately reflect, correct a possible interpretation, optionally learn from peers, and choose what to share. It earns **72/100 on the fixed weighted rubric** and **70/100 on the separate film-persuasion scale**. I would keep it in consideration, but not shortlist it on this film alone.

It merits consideration for its participant agency and candid labeling of prepared or disconnected features. Yet it does not prove model quality, peer reciprocity, or actual support delivery, and long static walkthroughs weaken its pace.

## First 15 seconds

The opening names “adults facing family pressure” and the dilemma “I can’t tell my family what I want without feeling ungrateful” (0–11s). Audience and tension are clear, but product differentiation waits until ~12s, when the interface offers private, correctable reflection before peer exploration and labels the journey fictional, prepared, and not model-connected.

## Scene audit

- **0–16s:** A strong first-person hook leads to a three-step path: “What Arjun said” → “A possibility to check” → “Arjun can correct it.” The viewer understands the basic idea, though the product promise could arrive sooner.
- **16–45s:** The private reflection keeps the original words visible. “Have I got this right?” is labeled a possible reading, with options to affirm, reject, or edit it. The film shows a correction and confirmed takeaway. This is a compelling agency mechanism, but the long, stable screens feel like a prepared walkthrough rather than a discovered interaction.
- **46–50s:** The edited line—“I can talk with my partner. It’s my parents. I want time to decide what I want.”—is the clearest emotional payoff. The film notes that the correction changes the words, not the ownership.
- **51–74s:** Day 4, Day 9, and Day 16 cards are labeled fictional, prepared historical examples, not live activity. One says “There are no replies yet.” This avoids false social proof, but shows authored examples rather than real community reciprocity.
- **77–100s:** “Ask Ravi in the group” previews the exact audience, alias, and question; it excludes the private conversation and journey. Consent is unchecked, the send control is disabled, and the interface says nothing is sent. This is strong privacy evidence, though the screen holds without further action and no completed share is shown.
- **105–116s:** The support page says no provider directory or credential checks are connected and no appointment can be requested. It is candid but ends in an unavailable pathway.
- **118–177s:** The film returns to prepared peer material, then closes on “I can ask for time before deciding.” The takeaway remains private and no next step is selected. This reinforces agency, but repeats the same state and disclosure over a long static ending.

## Authenticity, technical credibility, and Meta attribution

The film labels content fictional, prepared, illustrative, or local preview. It preserves original and corrected words, calls the interpretation a possible reading, and assigns choices to Arjun. Community cards are prepared examples with fictional people, no replies, and no live moderator. Provider checks and appointments are unavailable; group sharing is an unsent preview. The disclosures support credibility as a prototype, not live AI, social activity, provider integration, or deployment. Repeated cards feel authored rather than spontaneous.

At 35–45s, **Muse Spark** is proposed to organize a correctable reflection; optional voice transcription is also planned. This could help clarify a person’s words, but the film says no model is connected and uses prepared local rules. It shows no model output, transcription, reasoning, or performance. No model-execution credit is warranted; community content is peer material.

## Accessibility and film craft

Interface typography is high contrast and the core flow is legible. Dense community and journey pages require pausing. Captions remain visible but occupy a large lower band. Motion is minimal, which supports reading but creates long static stretches. Emotional intensity peaks at the opening dilemma, correction, and “ask for time” takeaway; the fictional person appears only through text, so the film asks viewers to imagine his presence. Audio limitations prevent judging narration or sound design.

## Five highest-impact fix tickets

1. **0–16s — Promise arrives late.** Replace the title-card sequence with a 12-second cold open: the dilemma plus “Harmonise helps you privately test and correct a reflection before choosing whether to hear peers.” Keep the fictional/no-model disclosure. **Expected:** audience, problem, and promise land within 15 seconds. **Verify:** a first-time viewer can state all three by 15s.
2. **16–45s — Interaction feels narrated.** Cut to original words, tentative reading, visible “Not quite” or edit action, and corrected text in one sequence. **Expected:** show the participant causing the change. **Verify:** action and changed state are visible in one uninterrupted shot.
3. **35–45s — Planned role could be missed.** Replace the small note with: “Planned: Muse Spark organizes a draft. Arjun decides, edits, or discards it. This preview uses prepared examples.” **Expected:** role and non-execution are unmistakable. **Verify:** viewers distinguish proposed AI from demonstrated behavior.
4. **51–74s — Peer value is static.** Show the confirmed theme, one fictional peer perspective, and unresolved/no-reply state together; retain the no-live-activity label. **Expected:** explain relevance without false social proof. **Verify:** viewers identify what is matched, illustrative, and live.
5. **139–177s — Ending repeats itself.** Replace the extended hold with “Keep privately,” “Offer one thing,” and “Maybe later,” then show the selected private option and one preview-only disclosure. **Expected:** close on agency rather than repeated disclaimer. **Verify:** chosen path and unsent status are readable without duplicate cards or dead air.

## Skeptical judge objection

“This is a thoughtful interface concept, but the film is mostly prepared copy and curated screens. It tells me how the product is meant to behave; it does not show a model doing the work, a real person using it, or a real community responding. The social and professional pathways are explicitly unavailable, so network benefit remains a hypothesis.”

## Best-case reaction

“Harmonise understands that someone facing family pressure may need space before advice. It offers a tentative reflection, gives the person editable ownership, lets them keep a takeaway private, and previews exactly what a peer question would disclose. The team labels prepared examples and unavailable integrations plainly, making the concept more trustworthy than an ambiguous AI demo.”

## Release recommendation and blockers

**Revise the film before release; do not present it as evidence of a live service.** It is defensible as a fictional, prepared prototype walkthrough. Visible blockers are: no connected model; fictional scenario; prepared, non-live community content; no live moderator; unavailable provider directory and credential checks; no appointment flow; and no message sent. Claims of live model, community, or provider functionality would exceed what the film shows.

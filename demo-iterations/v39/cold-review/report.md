# Blind cold-judge report — candidate.mp4

**Verdict: Maybe.** The film makes a specific, sensitive use case understandable and presents unusually clear privacy and consent boundaries. It also shows a meaningful correction loop that gives the user control over an interpretation. However, most screens explicitly label the material fictional, prepared, and disconnected; the community has no live members, and no AI model execution is evidenced. This is a credible frontend concept film, not proof of an operating service. I would keep it in consideration for a hackathon demo shortlist only if the category accepts a candid prototype preview.

## First 15 seconds

At 0:00, the opening title is “I can’t tell my family what I want without feeling ungrateful.” The panel names “For adults facing family pressure,” and the proposed promise is private reflection with choice over sharing. At 0:06–0:12, “Planned Muse Spark” is immediately qualified as “not connected” and “Prepared local text.” This distinguishes the intended role while limiting the claim. The opening does not explain the community contribution or distinguish the product from journaling and ordinary peer support.

## Scene audit

- **0:00–0:05:** The landing screen presents Arjun as a fictional scenario, the family-pressure problem, an adult audience, and “Start privately”/“Browse example stories.” The framing is compelling, though the browser UI sits small within a neutral canvas.
- **0:06–0:15:** A “thought to check” asks “Have I got this right?” and separates “What you said,” “Might matter,” and “What you want now.” The proposed reading is labeled as only a possible reading, with “Yes, that’s closer,” “Not quite,” and “Edit in my words.” This editability is the film’s strongest demonstration; captions are descriptive overlays, not evidence of live speech.
- **0:18–0:33:** Prepared conversation examples offer a possibility “not an answer,” and Arjun’s own wording is shown beside the changed, editable reflection. This conveys agency, but repeated holds slow the middle and risk making prepared material feel live unless viewers read each disclaimer.
- **0:36–0:50:** “Fictional community · prepared example · no live members” appears over sample member posts and a host statement that the host cannot decide for Arjun. The non-directive role is clear, but the cards and host line are scripted, not social activity; no real peers are present.
- **0:54–1:12:** The film shows a local draft to a fictional member, an editable single offered message, private conversation excluded, and a review/consent step. The disabled “Add to preview group” state is paired with “Nothing is sent from this preview.” The privacy boundary and consent gate are clear; no live send is shown. At 1:12–1:18, no provider is verified or bookable, a candid limitation.
- **1:20–1:34:** Arjun can keep a takeaway privately, offer one thing that helped, or choose “Maybe later.” “Maybe later selected” and “The private reflection stays private” make a low-pressure exit legible. This is a modest, fitting payoff, though the ending repeats the static choice screen.

The film establishes no real-person outcome, community usage, provider connection, model call, or service failure/recovery; any pitch should preserve these gaps.

## Authenticity, Meta attribution, and craft

The film repeatedly calls the material a “Fictional scenario,” “prepared example,” “no model connected,” “no live members,” and “no message service connected.” This is admirably candid, and the interface reinforces those distinctions. Apparent transformations—correcting a reflection, changing the message preview, and choosing to keep a takeaway private—are presented as example states, not witnessed user activity. The interface names “Muse Spark” as planned and says it may organize themes from words Arjun shares; it does not decide what is true or what to do. This is a plausible model role because intent is ambiguous and correction matters. But prepared local text substantiates only an interaction design, not model reasoning or execution. The community is also a proposed listening role; its dialogue is fictional.

Restrained colors, consistent typography and uncluttered cards support a calm tone. Captions summarize scenes, but repeated text-heavy screens slow pacing. No rapid motion is apparent. The tone is respectful but lacks a real voice or face; audio and spoken-caption quality are unjudgeable.

## Five highest-impact fix tickets

1. **0:00–0:15 — State the distinction sooner.** Replace the opening sequence with one concise line that pairs private reflection with an optional, user-edited peer learning path. Keep “fictional” and “prepared” in-frame. Verify with a cold viewer’s 15-second recall of audience, problem and distinction.
2. **0:18–0:33 — Reduce repeated states.** Replace the near-repeated reflection screens with one short sequence: initial wording, user correction, visibly changed reflection. Verify viewers can identify the change and consequence without pausing.
3. **0:36–0:50 — Make the community’s proposed value concrete.** Replace the static sample-card run with a single clearly labeled fictional peer response and a concise statement of what the host does. Verify viewers can name what is prepared and the human role.
4. **0:54–1:12 — Clarify the consent decision in one pass.** Replace the extended consent screens with a readable sequence showing exact text, named audience, excluded private context, unchecked consent, and disabled continuation; preserve the no-service disclaimer. Verify viewers can name the shared text and declined-consent outcome.
5. **1:20–1:34 — End on the agency payoff.** Replace repeated static holds with a brief selection of “Maybe later,” followed by a clean end card restating that nothing was shared. Verify viewers recall the private exit and next step.

## Judge reactions and recommendation

**Skeptical judge objection:** “The product’s most important promise depends on an AI reflection and peer community, yet this film shows neither a model call nor real peers. It is an attractive, carefully bounded mock flow; why should I score the central intelligence or network benefit as demonstrated?”

**Best-case reaction:** “This team understands the emotional risk in telling someone what they feel. It gives the person a way to correct the reflection, makes sharing specific and consent-gated, and treats ‘maybe later’ as a valid outcome. The presentation is unusually honest about what is still only a proposal.”

**Release recommendation: retain as a maybe, conditional on a prototype-preview track.** Explicit blockers to a stronger verdict are the absence of truthful live AI evidence, the absence of real community activity, and no observed outcome beyond a prepared scenario. These block claims of demonstrated execution and impact. Preserve the disclosures and show any newly real capability directly.
